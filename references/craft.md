# Craft: people, scenes, language, and narrative effects

Read the sections relevant to the user's problem. The prompts below are adaptable working tools, not mandatory character forms or rules of literary taste.

## Premise and progression

For a goal-driven story, a usable premise can connect a particular person, a situation, a desire, a difficulty, and a meaningful consequence. For another form, connect a subject, an organizing relationship or pattern, and the experience it opens for the audience. A premise should make subsequent choices possible.

Develop alternatives by changing the engine: who notices the event, what someone risks, whose interests clash, what is misunderstood, what is discovered, or how experiences are arranged. Renaming a character while preserving the same plot does not create a distinct direction.

For causal plotting, inspect an important transition as: established situation -> action or event -> response -> consequence -> changed possibilities. Coincidence can start or complicate a story; a convenient solution needs careful examination because it may bypass the work's established choices.

For accumulation or juxtaposition, inspect the relation between successive units: contrast, recurrence, variation, deepening intimacy, changed attention, or emerging context. Quiet work can progress through perception rather than confrontation.

## Character and relationship

Build only the character knowledge the writing needs. Useful questions include:

- What do they want in this situation, and why does it matter now?
- What do they believe, notice, misunderstand, avoid, or refuse?
- What options do their material circumstances actually permit?
- How do history, culture, status, work, and relationships shape behavior?
- What would they choose under the particular pressure of this scene?
- What does another character want from them, and what can each give or withhold?

Reveal these through selected action, speech, observation, interaction, or summary. Contradictory traits can make a person specific; contradictory facts need reconciliation. Distinguish a deliberate change in behavior from an unexplained change made for plot convenience.

An antagonist can be a person with coherent interests, an institution, an environment, an internal pattern, or a different source of pressure. Some forms need no antagonist. Avoid treating supporting characters as disposable tools whose knowledge and goals change whenever the protagonist needs help.

Track relationship changes in observable terms: trust, obligation, intimacy, authority, dependence, or knowledge. A changed relationship can supply an arc even when neither person undergoes a conventional transformation.

Character arcs may involve growth, deterioration, mixed change, resistance, stability, or an unresolved movement. Choose what fits the work rather than prescribing self-improvement.

Source for desire, portrayal, and consistency: [Open University: Portraying a character](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/history-the-arts/creative-writing/start-writing-fiction/content-section-1.4).

## Scene and summary

Before drafting or repairing a scene, establish its purpose in this work. It might change a situation, reveal a relationship, orient the audience, build atmosphere, stage a discovery, or make an ordinary experience newly perceptible.

For an active dramatic scene, identify who wants what now, what interferes, what tactics change, and what remains different afterward. For a reflective or atmospheric scene, identify the shifting attention, perception, or emotional relation. Do not manufacture an argument just to give it a conventional conflict.

Choose a plausible setting with opportunities, restrictions, and details the viewpoint can perceive. Establish enough spatial and temporal information for the audience to follow the important action. Let objects and surroundings affect behavior when useful.

Scene presents an experience moment by moment; summary compresses it. Use scene where a choice, encounter, or perception merits attention, and summary where compression serves pace or emphasis. Neither mode is intrinsically superior. A scene can contain summary, and a summary can contain telling detail.

Enter and leave at points that preserve needed orientation and create the intended rhythm. Removing every approach or aftermath can make a work feel abrupt; retaining every transition can make it drift.

For Swain-derived tools, read [Methods](methods.md), especially scene-sequel and MRUs.

## Dialogue and subtext

Write speech as an action within a relationship. A line can ask, evade, reassure, bargain, provoke, conceal, establish a boundary, or fail to do any of those successfully. Meaning also comes from what is answered, ignored, interrupted, or left unsaid.

Differentiate speakers by what they attend to, their vocabulary, rhythm, politeness, habits of evasion, and relationship to the listener. Do not rely on a catchphrase or phonetic accent alone.

When dialogue feels artificial:

1. Find information the speakers already know and are reciting only for the audience.
2. Find explanations of emotion that the interaction already conveys.
3. Identify each speaker's immediate purpose and revise the exchange around it.
4. Check whether silence, action, implication, summary, or a later reveal can do some of the work.

Subtext depends on an inferable relation between the spoken words and the situation. Making every line cryptic creates confusion rather than depth. Direct speech can be the right choice for honesty, urgency, instruction, or a character's voice.

## Narrator, viewpoint, and distance

Decide who tells the story, who perceives a particular moment, and what information can be supplied. Person, knowledge, reliability, and distance are separate choices.

- First person provides a particular speaker's account; it does not automatically make that speaker truthful or intimate.
- Second person can address a character, a reader, or a constructed participant; maintain the chosen function.
- Limited third person restricts access according to the chosen viewpoint.
- Omniscient narration can move beyond one person's knowledge, but its transitions and narrative authority still need deliberate handling.
- External narration can restrict itself to observable behavior without supplying private thoughts.

When repairing an accidental viewpoint jump, identify information that belongs to another mind and either externalize its evidence, mark a deliberate transition, or remove the unsupported access. Do not erase an intentional omniscient or multi-perspective design.

Narrative distance can move closer to sensation and thought or farther toward observation and summary. Use that movement to shape emphasis; inspect abrupt shifts that have no evident purpose.

Sources: [Living Handbook: Focalization](https://www-archiv.fdm.uni-hamburg.de/lhn/node/18.html), [Perspective and Point of View](https://www-archiv.fdm.uni-hamburg.de/lhn/node/26.html).

## Time, order, and information

Keep the chronology of events distinct from the order of presentation. For complicated structures, track both. A flashback should arrive where its information or experience matters; it need not explain everything that happened earlier.

Use duration deliberately: linger, compress, pause, omit, or repeat according to the intended effect. When presenting the same event again, make clear what the new viewpoint, context, or emphasis contributes.

Design information around the audience's experience:

- Suspense can arise from anticipating a consequential outcome.
- Curiosity can arise from wanting to understand a gap or prior cause.
- Surprise can change expectations through unexpected information.
- Dramatic irony can arise when the audience understands something a character does not.

These are useful distinctions for design, not exclusive categories. Withholding information should still leave enough context to understand what is at stake or why the gap is interesting. Do not hide a viewpoint character's ordinary knowledge in an implausible way solely to force a reveal.

For a twist, identify what changes in interpretation, which earlier details remain true, and what evidence supports the new reading. Check that the surprise does not contradict established facts or rely on an unintroduced exception.

Source on sequence and reception: [Living Handbook: Sequentiality](https://www-archiv.fdm.uni-hamburg.de/lhn/node/91.html).

## Voice, detail, and rhythm

Choose detail through the narrator's or character's attention. Specificity comes from a meaningful selection, not a quota of smells, colors, adjectives, or metaphors. In nonfiction, every factual observation must have a basis.

Use sentence and paragraph rhythm to manage attention, pace, hesitation, and emphasis. Read for cumulative effect. Uniform short sentences can be as monotonous as uniform long ones.

When editing the user's voice, first identify what makes it distinctive: diction, syntax, humor, intimacy, restraint, region, or oral quality. Preserve deliberate choices. Offer an optional change as an option when it is a matter of taste rather than a demonstrated problem.

An image, motif, repetition, or callback should contribute meaning or experience. Do not add ornate language merely to signal literary quality. Showing, telling, abstraction, and direct emotional statements can all be useful in context.

## Theme and ending

Find theme in patterns of choice, consequence, image, relationship, or inquiry. An explicit statement can serve a particular form, but a story need not end with a lesson. Do not flatten moral complexity into a slogan.

Decide what kind of ending the brief wants: resolved action, altered relationship, a resonant image, an answer, a deliberate uncertainty, or a continuation. Connect it to the experience the work has established. Open-ended does not mean arbitrary; a quiet ending does not need a late catastrophe.

After an ending revision, inspect earlier preparation and later implications. A payoff may be emotional or perceptual rather than a puzzle solution. Preserve purposeful unanswered questions and identify accidental loose ends.
