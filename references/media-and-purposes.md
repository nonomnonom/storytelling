# Fit the narrative to its medium and purpose

Use the applicable section, not every section. Genre, medium, audience, cultural tradition, and factual status are different dimensions. A horror audio drama, a factual comic, and an interactive relationship story need different combinations of decisions.

## Prose: flash fiction, short stories, novels, and serials

Use narration, viewpoint, interiority, selected detail, and sentence rhythm as resources. Scale the number of people, settings, turns, and threads to the requested length. A short work can concentrate on an encounter, perception, or decision without a compressed feature-film outline.

For a novel or serial, track established facts, chronology, viewpoint knowledge, unresolved threads, and relationship changes. Reuse a project's current continuity notes; create only what sustained work needs. Each installment can provide a local experience while supporting larger developments. A cliffhanger is an option, not a compulsory ending.

For long requests beyond a single response's practical capacity, state a concrete delivery scope and produce useful work within it. Do not label an outline or opening chapter as a completed novel. Respect the user's chosen serialization or drafting plan.

## Film, television, animation, and video

Translate story into what an audience can see and hear. Use observable action, dialogue, sound, framing, sequence, and performance opportunities. Private thoughts need a deliberate device if their exact content must reach the audience.

Write the requested artifact: treatment, beat outline, scene, screenplay, storyboard captions, or narration. Use screenplay formatting when requested; do not insert camera instructions into every line unless the production needs them. Production tools and animation engines are outside this skill's mechanics.

For a time-limited video, estimate pacing from words, action, silence, cuts, and delivery rate, then label the estimate. Actual runtime needs performance or playback. Preserve a meaningful ending rather than spending the entire duration on setup.

For adaptation, identify the experience to retain and the mechanisms the new medium can carry. A literal transfer of paragraphs may lose their function. Explain consequential changes in viewpoint, chronology, condensation, or omitted material when useful.

## Stage, performance, and musical forms

Consider bodies, space, entrances, exits, audience attention, scene changes, and the resources of the specified production. Write playable intentions and interactions. A stage direction should convey what the production needs without prescribing every gesture by default.

For a musical or song narrative, determine what singing changes: a decision, an emotional relation, a perspective, an elapsed period, or a recurring idea. When the task concerns actual music, keep lyric writing distinct from composition and measured musical timing. Do not claim an audio performance was reviewed without hearing it.

For a cultural performance form, use [Research and traditions](research-and-traditions.md) before adopting its specific conventions.

## Comics, manga, and sequential visual work

Plan what each panel shows, what its text adds, and how the sequence carries time and information. Consider page or screen flow, readable text density, silent panels, and what a reveal needs to be withheld until the next unit.

Avoid assigning several incompatible moments to one static panel. An action can be split across panels or represented through a selected instant. Preserve the distinction between what is depicted, what is narrated, and what the reader infers between images.

For visual-only storytelling, test whether important action and relationships can be understood without explanatory captions. If artwork is not available, deliver a panel or shot plan and distinguish that from rendered proof.

## Audio drama, podcasts, and oral storytelling

Make orientation audible: who speaks, what changes, and which sounds matter. Distinguish narration, dialogue, sound cues, and silence. Use sound to convey narrative information rather than adding a constant decorative bed.

For oral performance, write for breath, delivery, listener memory, and the actual setting. Repeated phrasing can help an audience follow a spoken story. Do not mechanically convert speech into formal written prose.

The Moth is a specific practice of true personal storytelling, not the definition of all oral traditions. When requested, consult its guidance about stakes, a clear direction, and telling rather than simply reading a text. Source: [The Moth's tips](https://www.themoth.org/tell-your-story/storytelling-tips-tricks).

## Interactive fiction, games, roleplay, and simulations

Define the player's role, meaningful choices, available information, and consequences. Use a design appropriate to the requested control:

| Model | Use | Main consistency concern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Branching | Alternatives lead to different subsequent content or outcomes | Branch growth, continuity, and distinct consequences |
| Branch and reconverge | Different experiences return to shared content | Carry meaningful prior state into shared scenes |
| Storylets / quality-based | Modular content unlocks from state or attributes | Preconditions, updates, repetition, and accessibility |
| Salience-based | The system selects content most relevant to current conditions | Rule priority, ties, and appropriate selections |
| Waypoints | Flexible activity moves through selected narrative milestones | Required transitions and progress without trapping the player |
| Environmental narrative | Locations, objects, and traces convey events | Legibility, consistency, and optional discovery |
| Emergent narrative | Play and interacting systems produce narratable events | Do not promise an authored arc that the system cannot guarantee |

For a small interactive design, specify only the needed state, transitions, and endings. At each choice, distinguish presentation from state changes. Do not invalidate a meaningful choice through unacknowledged convergence or assume that every cosmetic choice needs a separate ending.

Trace relevant paths after editing: are conditions reachable, is required information available, do variables remain consistent, and can the player progress? Check loops for deliberate exits or ongoing play. Use an available runtime when implementation is requested; a written path review is not an executed playtest.

For roleplay, respect the user's control over their own character and the agreed play boundaries. Present situations and consequences without taking over their decisions unless the requested simulation gives the agent that role.

Sources: [inkle's interactive writing tutorial](https://www.inklestudios.com/ink/web-tutorial/), [Emily Short on narrative structures](https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/), [Harvey Smith and Matthias Worch on environmental narrative](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1012712/Environmental-Narrative-Your-World-is).

## Documentary, journalism, memoir, biography, and history

Start with the evidence and the user's material. Distinguish verified records, attributed recollection, inference, uncertainty, reconstruction, and invention. Memory is a source with limits, not automatic proof of exact dialogue or chronology.

Choose a structure the evidence can support. A scene requires a basis for its people, place, action, and quoted speech. When the evidence supports only a summary, use summary. Do not supply weather, gestures, smells, private thoughts, or motives because they seem likely.

A narrative can organize an inquiry or change in understanding instead of a protagonist's dramatic arc. Editing chronology for clarity must not imply a false sequence or causal relation. Transparent hypothetical examples belong outside the factual account unless the form explicitly accommodates them.

The hourglass is one reporting structure: an informative opening, a transition, then a narrative account. It is useful when readers need important facts promptly as well as an account of how events unfolded. Sources: [Poynter on the hourglass](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2003/the-hourglass-serving-the-news-serving-the-reader/), [Roy Peter Clark on fact and fiction](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2004/09/07/the-line-between-fact-and-fiction-3/).

## Business, brand, education, science, and data

Determine the actual communication objective: understanding, remembering, explaining, persuading, or making a decision. A narrative is useful only if it serves that objective. Some material is clearer as a direct explanation or comparison.

- **StoryBrand:** use the customer's problem, a guide, a plan, action, and outcomes when requested or appropriate for brand communication. Do not invent a customer success story. Source: [Official material](https://storybrand.com/downloads/your-brand-is-not-the-hero.pdf).
- **Duarte's presentation pattern:** contrast the current situation with a supported possible future and lead toward a clear action or changed understanding. Source: [Duarte on business storytelling](https://www.duarte.com/resources/communication-skills/business-storytelling/).
- **ABT:** connect context with a complication and a consequential response using And, But, Therefore as a compression tool. Do not invent causality merely to fill the template. Source: [ABT workshop material](https://abtframework.com/nailing-it-workshop-round-1/).

In educational work, distinguish a fictional teaching scenario from evidence about the real world. For science and data, preserve uncertainty, denominators, comparisons, dates, and the distinction between correlation and causation. An engaging story cannot turn a weak finding into a strong claim. Use the relevant factual expertise and sources when the subject requires them.

## Genre and audience promises

Treat genre as an expectation to investigate or negotiate, not a label that replaces the brief. These are design questions, not universal definitions:

| Intended experience | Questions to ask of the work |
| --- | --- |
| Mystery / investigation | What question is pursued? What evidence can the audience assess? How is the eventual explanation supported? |
| Thriller / suspense | What consequential outcome is anticipated? How do time, pressure, and information affect it? |
| Horror | What experience of dread, fear, disturbance, or uncertainty is intended? What makes its source particular? |
| Romance / relationship | What relationship is central? What changes its possibilities? Which ending expectation has the user chosen? |
| Comedy / satire | What expectation, status, pattern, or assumption is being played with? What is the target and the intended effect? |
| Fantasy / science fiction | Which world rules affect events? What must be understood now? Are consequences consistent with those rules? |
| Historical narrative | Which details need verification? Which are invention? How do actual conditions shape the people involved? |
| Slice of life / literary / experimental | What organizes attention? What changes through accumulation, language, juxtaposition, or perception? |
| Children's or family work | What age, reading/listening context, and emotional complexity are intended? Does the work respect the audience's intelligence? |
| Myth, folktale, or tradition-based work | Which specific tradition and version are involved? What does the source or practitioner establish? |

When a specific genre convention, platform format, or unfamiliar medium matters, verify it rather than presenting a personal preference as an industry rule. Support intentional hybrid forms and departures from convention.
