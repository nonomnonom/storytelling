# Story methods: selection and application

Use this reference when structure, development, or method choice is the problem. Choose by purpose, scale, and the work already on the page. These summaries are original operational interpretations, not substitutes for the full source methods.

## Select by the problem

| Need | Start with | Check before committing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Events feel arbitrary | Causal links and consequences | Does each important event have a plausible cause or meaningful response? |
| A goal-driven middle drifts | Three-act, seven-point, or scene-level diagnosis | Does pressure change tactics or the available choices? |
| A character's journey lacks shape | Journey models or character-led development | Does this work actually involve departure, transformation, or return? |
| An idea is too vague to draft | Snowflake or a short scene exploration | Does expansion create decisions and relationships rather than background volume? |
| The ending feels unrelated | MICE-style thread tracking or reverse outlining | What did the opening lead the audience to expect? |
| A quiet narrative feels shapeless | Accumulation, juxtaposition, framing, or episodic progression | What changes in perception, relationship, attention, or pattern? |
| The user wants a reveal-led form | Information design or kishotenketsu where appropriate | Does the new material alter how earlier material is understood? |
| A scene feels inert | Scene purpose, scene-sequel, or Story Grid | What experience or change does this scene produce? |
| A factual story lacks evidence | Research and transparent gaps | Is the proposed structural beat supported by actual events? |
| A game becomes hard to maintain | Branches, storylets, or waypoint design | Which choices must have persistent consequences? |

## Dramatic and journey models

### Aristotle's Poetics

Use as an analytical lens for a unified action, causal coherence, reversal, recognition, and the relation between events and emotional effect. Its central context is tragedy and epic, not a universal modern screenplay formula.

Application: identify the central action; test whether important turns arise plausibly from the established situation; inspect whether a discovery or reversal changes the action rather than merely adding information. Do not force all stories into tragic unity or equate Aristotle with later beat sheets.

Source: [Poetics, translated by S. H. Butcher](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/poetics.html).

### Freytag's dramatic construction

Use to inspect the rise and fall of dramatic action: introduction, rise, climax, fall, and catastrophe in Freytag's original dramatic context. Contemporary classroom diagrams often simplify or reinterpret it.

Application: locate the dramatic high point and the consequences that follow. Be clear whether using the historical model or a modern adaptation. Do not label every beginning-middle-end outline as Freytag, or require the climax at the same location in every medium.

Source: [Die Technik des Dramas](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/50616).

### Syd Field's paradigm / three-act structure

Use for a story whose setup establishes a direction, whose middle develops confrontation, and whose resolution answers the resulting dramatic problem. Plot points change the direction of the action.

Application: describe the story's opening situation, the event that commits it to a new course, the middle's development, the later turn toward resolution, and the outcome. Derive scene length and timing from the actual medium and brief; page percentages are planning aids when relevant.

Source: [The Paradigm Worksheet](https://sydfield.com/syd_resources/the-paradigm-worksheet/).

### Campbell's monomyth / Hero's Journey

Use when departure, initiation, and return illuminate the intended mythic or transformational experience. Campbell's comparative theory and a writing template are different uses of the material.

Application: establish what is left behind, what is encountered or learned, what it costs, and what returning means. A return can be psychological or relational when that reading fits the work. Do not claim Campbell's account is a proven law of all cultures or that every stage must occur.

Source: [Joseph Campbell Foundation](https://www.jcf.org/learn/joseph-campbell-heros-journey).

### Vogler's Writer's Journey

Use a twelve-stage map when the user wants a practical journey-based development aid: ordinary world, call, refusal, mentor, threshold, tests, approach, ordeal, reward, road back, resurrection, and return with the elixir.

Application: explain the function a stage serves in this story; combine, move, or omit stages when appropriate. Archetypal functions need not become separate stereotyped characters. Distinguish Vogler's adaptation from Campbell's original analysis.

Source: [The Writer's Journey, third edition](https://www.tlu.ee/~rajaleid/montaazh/Christopher%20Vogler%20-%20Writers%20Journey.pdf).

### Dan Harmon's Story Circle

Use a compact cycle for a character who wants something, enters unfamiliar circumstances, adapts, obtains something, pays a price, returns, and changes.

Application: test the connection between wanting, obtaining, and paying. A meaningful cost should affect the resolution. Do not add a journey home to a story whose form does not need it.

Source: [Harmon's Story Structure 101, archived on Channel 101 Wiki](https://channel101.fandom.com/wiki/Story_Structure_101%3A_Super_Basic_Shit). This is an archive of the author's tutorial, not the current original site.

### Save the Cat!

Use when the user asks for this method or needs a detailed dramatic beat map. The fifteen labels are Opening Image, Theme Stated, Set-up, Catalyst, Debate, Break into 2, B Story, Fun and Games, Midpoint, Bad Guys Close In, All Is Lost, Dark Night of the Soul, Break into 3, Finale, and Final Image.

Application: map existing material to the functions before adding scenes. Examine what the midpoint changes and how the finale grows from the earlier experience. Do not mechanically pad short works or replace the user's tone with a commercial-film default.

Source: [Official Beat Sheet Mapper](https://savethecat.com/beat-mapper).

### Seven-point structure

Use the version taught by Dan Wells for a concise plan: hook, first plot turn, first pinch, midpoint, second pinch, second plot turn, and resolution.

Application: work backward from the ending when that clarifies the contrast with the hook; design the midpoint as a meaningful change in the protagonist's approach. A pinch creates pressure that matters to the story, not necessarily a physical attack. This is one framework, not evidence that all narratives have seven compulsory parts.

Source: [Writing Excuses 7.41 with Dan Wells](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-7-41-seven-point-story-structure/).

### Michael Hauge's six-stage approach

Use for a protagonist pursuing a clear goal with a defined endpoint: setup, new situation, progress, complications and higher stakes, final push, and aftermath.

Application: connect the outer goal to the emotional experience when relevant. Hauge explicitly identifies the goal-driven condition of this approach. Adapt it to length rather than insisting on feature-film timing.

Source: [Hauge on novel and screenplay structure](https://storymastery.com/novel-structure-vs-screenplay-structure/).

### John Truby's Anatomy of Story

Use when the user requests Truby or when character relationships and the story's moral choices need coordinated development. His system includes a seven-step foundation and a larger twenty-two-step development.

Application at the foundation: relate weakness/need, desire, opponent, plan, battle, self-revelation, and new equilibrium. Inspect how the opponent pressures the central character's weakness and how the character's choices embody the theme. If asked for the full twenty-two-step breakdown, consult the source rather than inventing step names or ordering from memory. Do not require a didactic moral verdict.

Sources: [Truby's premise and seven-step videos](https://truby.com/video/), [Anatomy of Story class](https://truby.com/anatomy-of-story-audio-class/).

### Jill Chamberlain's Nutshell Technique

Use when requested for a screenplay or when examining the connection between a protagonist's flaw and the situation that tests it. It is a distinct system, not a renamed three-act outline.

Application: read the creator's material or worksheet before supplying a formal Nutshell analysis. Preserve its terms and relationships; identify a general character diagnosis as such if the full source is unavailable. Do not fabricate a complete method from the title or a sales page.

Source: [The Screenplay Workshop with Jill Chamberlain](https://www.thescreenplayworkshop.org/nutshell-technique/).

## Development and diagnostic methods

### Snowflake Method

Expand a small design into a more detailed one: a concise premise, a paragraph of progression, character summaries, longer synopsis, richer character work, a scene list, and drafting. Read the original for its formal ten-step version.

Application: develop only as much detail as makes the next writing decision possible. Return to an earlier level when a later step exposes a contradiction. The author permits selecting useful steps; do not require ten administrative deliverables for a short story.

Source: [Randy Ingermanson's original article](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/).

### MICE Quotient

Identify threads centered on milieu, idea/inquiry, character, and event. Examine what opens each thread and what would close it. Several thread types can coexist.

Application: track which question, disturbance, personal condition, or entry into a setting gives the audience an expectation. An ending can deliberately leave a thread open, but make that an understood choice. Do not assume the author wants every character transformed.

Source: [Writing Excuses 6.10 with Orson Scott Card](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-6-10-scott-cards-m-i-c-e-quotient/).

### Story Grid's five components

Use inciting incident, turning-point progressive complication, crisis, climax, and resolution as a lens on a dramatic unit. In this system, the crisis is a consequential dilemma and the climax is the enacted answer.

Application: identify the destabilization, failed or altered approach, real alternatives, actual choice, and resulting change. Missing causal connections matter more than whether five headings can be filled. Its universal claims belong to the system; do not impose them as a law on quiet, experimental, or non-dramatic work.

Source: [Story Grid's Five Commandments](https://storygrid.com/articles/five-commandments-of-storytelling).

### Scene-sequel and motivation-reaction units

The Swain-derived scene pattern relates goal, conflict, and setback; the sequel pattern relates reaction, dilemma, and decision. An MRU connects an external stimulus to a character's response at a smaller scale.

Application: when action seems arbitrary, restore the link between what happens, what the viewpoint character perceives, and what they decide. Reactions can be compressed or implied. These tools do not require every scene to end in disaster or every response to follow a visible three-part checklist.

Sources: [Ingermanson's interpretation of Swain](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/writing-the-perfect-scene/), [his later clarification](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/blog/2009/05/26/more-on-scenes-and-sequels-2/).

### Discovery writing and reverse outlining

Use discovery writing when exploratory drafting will reveal voice, relationships, or possibilities more effectively than advance plotting. After drafting, describe what each unit actually does; use that reverse outline to find gaps, repetition, misplaced information, or an emerging pattern.

Application: retain productive discoveries, then revise the structure around them. A reverse outline is an observation of the current draft, not the original plan copied onto it.

Source for reverse outlining and revision: [George Mason University Writing Center](https://writingcenter.gmu.edu/writing-resources/writing-as-process/revising). Its examples concern essays; the scene-level adaptation here is a writing practice, not the source's formal fiction method.

## Alternative organizing principles

### Kishotenketsu

Use introduction, development, a turn or contrasting material, and a concluding relation when juxtaposition or reinterpretation is useful. The turn can alter the significance of prior material; it need not be a shocking reveal.

Application: identify the relation made visible by the last part. Do not assert that stories using this form cannot contain conflict or that it represents all East Asian narratives. Chinese, Japanese, and Korean usages deserve their own context when the distinction matters.

Source: [Kishotenketsu as Leitmotif, Journal of Sound and Music in Games](https://online.ucpress.edu/jsmg/article/4/4/15/197773/Kishotenketsu-as-LeitmotifStorytelling-and-Musical).

### Jo-ha-kyu

Study as a principle of development and tempo in Noh, from an opening through development toward a faster conclusion. It can operate at different scales within a performance.

Application: consider changes in pace, density, and movement when shaping a performance. Do not reduce it to another three-act plot template or claim familiarity with a Noh tradition from its name alone.

Source: [Stanford Noh Project: Form](https://noh.stanford.edu/form/).

### Le Guin's Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction

Use as a way to reconsider a story organized around relationships, gathering, ordinary activities, survival, or many contained experiences rather than one dominating heroic action. It is an essay and a narrative perspective, not a fixed beat sheet.

Application: choose what the narrative holds together and what relationships or patterns become perceptible through accumulation. A quieter story still needs deliberate selection, sequence, and attention. Do not add a villain merely to satisfy a dramatic template.

Source: [Ursula K. Le Guin's official site](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/the-carrier-bag-theory-of-fiction).

### Propp's Morphology of the Folktale

Use as an analytical account of functions in the folktales Propp studied. It can help inspect recurring actions and roles or develop a consciously related folktale form.

Application: learn the functions from the source when a formal analysis is requested. Explain the corpus and scope; do not call it a universal formula for world folklore or require all functions in every tale.

Source: [University of Texas Press](https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292783768/morphology-of-the-folktale/).

### Other formal arrangements

An episodic, braided, framed, circular, fragmentary, or parallel arrangement can solve a problem without a named authorial method. State the relation that organizes the units: chronology, location, repeated encounter, theme, contrast, inquiry, or a changing relationship. Test what the audience learns from this order that another order would lose.

For an unfamiliar named method, verify its source, version, function, and limitations. Add only the knowledge needed for the user's work. Do not claim a complete worldwide inventory, invent an attribution, or pad an outline with framework vocabulary.
