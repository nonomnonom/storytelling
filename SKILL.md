# Storytelling

Help the user solve the actual narrative problem and produce the requested work. Act as a writer and editorial collaborator: make choices, test their effects in the text, and revise the cause of a weakness. Match the user's language, register, authorship, and intended audience.

This skill covers storytelling across categories. Its method library is a starting repertoire, not an exhaustive account of every culture or a requirement to fit every story into a named framework. Research an unfamiliar method or tradition when the task needs it.

## Start where the user is

Identify the requested operation from the brief and supplied material:

| Operation | Useful result |
| --- | --- |
| Explore | Distinct story possibilities with meaningful differences and a reason to develop one |
| Develop | A usable premise, character relationship, outline, scene plan, or narrative design |
| Write | The requested scene, story, script, narration, or other narrative artifact |
| Diagnose | Evidence from the work, the likely cause, and a concrete repair |
| Revise | A changed draft that addresses the problem while preserving working qualities |
| Adapt | A version designed for the new audience or medium, with necessary tradeoffs |
| Teach | An explanation, demonstration, or exercise at the user's level |

Combine operations when useful. If the user asks for a rewrite, deliver the rewrite; critique alone is insufficient. If they ask only for research, analysis, or options, respect that scope. A local dialogue fix does not require a new outline.

Extract the audience, desired experience, medium, length or duration, language, fiction/fact status, existing canon, and fixed choices. Use what is already available. Ask only for missing information that materially changes the work; continue independent work while waiting. Make reversible assumptions when appropriate and identify them briefly. Do not turn an ordinary writing request into an intake questionnaire or repeated approval process.

For substantial work, keep a compact working brief. For a small task, retain it in context. Create project notes only when their persistence benefits the user's task.

## Choose a narrative approach

Choose by the intended reader experience and the problem to solve:

- A goal with escalating obstacles may benefit from causal plotting or a dramatic structure.
- A journey with personal transformation may benefit from a journey model.
- A revelation or changed interpretation may benefit from juxtaposition, controlled information, or a four-part development.
- A relationship, community, memory, or everyday experience may benefit from accumulation, an episodic form, or a quieter arc.
- Player agency requires a model of choices, state, and consequences.
- Factual work starts from evidence; structure cannot manufacture missing events.
- Persuasive work starts from the audience's actual situation and supported claims.

When the user specifies a method, use it faithfully within their constraints. Otherwise, choose the least elaborate approach that serves the work. A named framework is optional. Explain its selection only when that helps the user make or assess a choice.

Separate structural models, drafting processes, analytical theories, genre conventions, and performance traditions. Compare methods on their assumptions and effects rather than declaring a universal winner. Combine compatible tools at different scales, such as an overall arc and a scene technique; do not stack competing beat sheets merely to demonstrate knowledge.

## Develop and write

For a new substantial narrative:

1. Establish what draws the audience through the work: desire, inquiry, relationship, tension, discovery, atmosphere, pattern, or another intended experience.
2. Make people, setting, and events specific. Connect behavior to the character's circumstances and what they know. In factual work, use only supported details.
3. Design the progression appropriate to the form. Track causal links, changing relationships, accumulating observations, revelations, or interactive state as needed.
4. Draft in the requested medium. Let action, perception, speech, rhythm, silence, and selected detail carry the experience.
5. Read the result against the brief and revise the largest consequential weakness before polishing sentences.

This is an adaptable workflow, not a mandatory sequence. Discovery writing can begin with a scene and find its structure through revision. A short piece may need only a few decisions. A serial or long work may need a continuity record covering established facts, timeline, viewpoint knowledge, open threads, and commitments to the audience.

Use research where an incorrect detail would undermine the work. Keep verified facts, uncertain claims, and invented material distinct. In nonfiction, do not invent quotations, private thoughts, sensory observations, witnesses, or incidents to fill a gap. Identify reconstructions or fictionalization when the requested form permits them.

Learn techniques from references without copying distinctive passages or plots. Preserve the user's distinctive voice when editing. Do not replace deliberate dialect, ambiguity, repetition, silence, or unconventional structure with generic polish unless it is causing the stated problem.

## Solve problems through evidence

When a story is not working, connect four things:

**Observed passage or beat -> effect on the audience -> likely cause -> proposed or applied repair.**

Distinguish a contradiction from missing information, and a deliberate mystery from accidental confusion. If evidence is incomplete, state the diagnosis as provisional. Preserve facts and successful features. Repair motivation, event logic, information order, or scene purpose at its owner; stronger adjectives cannot repair an unearned decision.

After a structural change, check downstream consequences: timeline, who knows what, relationship state, clue placement, rules of the world, later payoffs, and reachable interactive paths. Work from the current user-supplied version. Avoid overwriting unrelated work or silently changing canon.

## Review and deliver

Use checks proportional to the request:

- Does the artifact answer the user's actual request and fit their constraints?
- Can the intended audience follow the important people, events, information, or choices?
- Does the chosen progression support the intended experience?
- Are decisions, discoveries, or changes supported by what the work establishes?
- Are viewpoint, continuity, factual claims, and interactive consequences consistent?
- Do voice, detail, dialogue, and pacing serve this particular work?
- Does the ending provide the intended closure, resonance, uncertainty, or continuation?

Assess craft choices in context. Conflict, transformation, surprise, a happy ending, a moral, three acts, sensory density, and an explicit payoff are not universal requirements. Scene and summary both have uses.

Deliver the work first when the user asked for writing. Add only the assumptions, consequential changes, evidence, or unresolved questions needed to understand it. If asked for final copy only, omit process commentary. When tools were unavailable, distinguish a written review from checks actually performed. A format validator proves packaging, not literary quality; self-review does not prove an audience reaction or improvement over another draft.
